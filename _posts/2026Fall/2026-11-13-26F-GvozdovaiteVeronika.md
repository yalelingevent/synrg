<p>In this talk I will consider the interaction between (i) verbal structure in deverbal nominals and (ii) the interpretation of these nominals at LF. Borer (2013) makes a generalization connecting (i) and (ii): entity-referring (non-compositional) meanings cannot arise in the presence of S-functors. I present evidence against this generalization from Lithuanian nominalizations. This appears to pose a problem for a homonymity analysis of nominalization ambiguity (Borer 2013), but perhaps not for an allosemy-based account, where R-meanings are not, strictly speaking, non-compositional (Wood 2023). I work out some predictions that an allosemy-based approach makes for both entity-referring (R) nominals and complex event nominals (CENs). Using data from Lithuanian and English, I present two arguments in favor of the phrasal-embedding approach, where R-nominals are idioms and CENs introduce their internal argument in the verbal layer of their structure. The first argument comes from R-interpretations of nominalizations with two S-functors. I argue that the meaning of these nominals cannot be reduced to the meaning of the root in combination with the nominalizer – instead, the S-functors play a non-vacuous role in R-interpretation. An allosemy-based theory of nominalization ambiguity has to reckon with this observation. 
The second argument comes from CENs, specifically the interpretation of their internal argument. The locus of the difference between the CEN and R interpretations in an allosemous approach is in how <i>v</i> is interpreted: in a CEN, <i>v</i> gets an alloseme containing the Theme predicate. Following Schein (2017), I argue that the introduction of the thematic predicate has to be separated from the eventive predicate, even in nominals. This has the syntactic consequence that <i>λxλe</i>.Theme(<i>x</i>,<i>e</i>) must be introduced as a constituent with the argument DP in the verbal syntactic layer, i.e. against Delayed Gratification (Myler 2014 and subsequent) cross-categorially.</p>
